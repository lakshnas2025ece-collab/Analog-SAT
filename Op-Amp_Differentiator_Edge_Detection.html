Introduction and fundamentals

Problem statement

An instrumentation system needs to identify rapid changes in an input waveform. Develop an op-amp differentiator that converts each fast edge into a distinct output pulse, so that a following comparator can flag it.

Definition

An op-amp differentiator is an inverting amplifier circuit whose output voltage is proportional to the time derivative of its input voltage. A capacitor sits in series with the input and a resistor sits in the feedback path. Ideally:

Vo(t) = − Rf C · dVin(t)/dt

A constant input gives zero output. A fast edge gives a large output. This is exactly what an edge detector needs, because the output is large only while the input is changing.

Operating principle in one paragraph

Negative feedback holds the inverting node at about 0 V (virtual ground). The input capacitor therefore sees the full input voltage and passes a current i = C·dVin/dt. The op-amp draws almost no input current, so this entire current flows through Rf. The output must swing to −iRf to keep the node at 0 V. The output is therefore proportional to the rate of change of the input.

Why it matters

  • It is the standard analog way to detect edges, slopes and rates of change (dV/dt).
  • It is the "D" term of analog PID controllers and a building block of waveform-shaping and trigger circuits.
  • It is a textbook case of the trade-off between ideal behaviour and noise and stability in real circuits, which is why GATE and interviews test it often.

Prerequisites

  • Inverting op-amp configuration, virtual ground, ideal op-amp assumptions.
  • Capacitor relation i = C dv/dt and impedance 1/(sC).
  • Laplace transform basics, poles and zeros, Bode plots (dB per decade).
  • Slew rate, gain–bandwidth product, input offset voltage and bias current.

Working principle and detailed explanation

Step-by-step operation of the ideal circuit

Virtual ground. With high open-loop gain and negative feedback, V− ≈ V+ = 0 V.
Capacitor current. The capacitor voltage equals Vin, so iC = C dVin/dt flows toward the inverting node.
No current into the op-amp. The input current is about zero, so iC flows entirely through Rf toward the output.
Output voltage. Vo = 0 − iCRf = −RfC dVin/dt.
Signal flow for an edge. A rising edge (dVin/dt > 0) gives a negative spike. A falling edge gives a positive spike. Flat parts give about 0 V.

Input-to-output behaviour

InputIdeal outputReason
DC level0 VdV/dt = 0; the capacitor blocks DC.
Linear ramp, slope kConstant −RfCkDerivative of a ramp is constant.
Sine, Vm sin ωt−ωRfC Vm cos ωt (lags input by 90°)Derivative of sine is cosine; the inversion contributes 180°.
Triangle waveSquare waveConstant slope of opposite sign on each half-cycle.
Square waveNarrow alternating spikes at the edgesZero slope except at the edges. An ideal edge would give an infinite impulse; real edges and the op-amp limit the spike.

DC and AC behaviour

DC

The capacitor is an open circuit, so there is no DC path from the input. The op-amp sees a voltage-follower-like noise gain of 1 at DC. The DC output is therefore the input offset voltage Vos plus bias-current effects:

Vo,DC ≈ Vos ± IosRf (with Rb = Rf)

AC

Gain magnitude rises with frequency, |H| = ωRfC, and the phase is −90°. In the practical circuit the gain peaks near fa and then falls at −20 dB/decade. Differentiation is accurate only well below fa.

Why the ideal circuit fails in practice

  • Noise. The gain keeps rising +20 dB/decade, so high-frequency noise is amplified most.
  • Instability. The noise gain 1 + sRfC rises at +20 dB/decade while the op-amp open-loop gain falls at −20 dB/decade. They cross with a closing slope of 40 dB/decade, giving almost no phase margin: ringing or oscillation.
  • Capacitive source loading. With no Rs, the source drives a pure capacitor, which is difficult for many sources and for the op-amp's own input.
  • Saturation. A very fast edge demands a huge Vo and drives the output into the rails.

The practical fix

Adding Rs in series with C and Cf across Rf gives:

H(s) = − sRfC / [ (1 + sRsC)(1 + sRfCf) ]

The numerator provides the differentiation. Each pole bends the gain curve downward. With RsC = RfCf the two poles coincide at fa = fb, so the gain rises at +20 dB/decade, peaks at Rf/(2Rs) near fa, then falls at −20 dB/decade.

Important assumptions

  • Ideal op-amp (infinite gain and bandwidth, zero input current) except where stated.
  • Linear operation: the output stays within about ±11 V and the slew rate is not exceeded.
  • Differentiation holds only for f ≪ fa. Beyond that the circuit behaves as a band-limited amplifier.

Design procedure for the edge detector

Fix the largest edge. Amplitude ΔV = 2 V, edge time tr = 20 µs, so the maximum slope is ΔV/tr = 105 V/s.
Pick C and Rf. The ideal peak is RfC·ΔV/tr. With C = 10 nF and Rf = 10 kΩ, RfC = 100 µs, so the ideal peak is 10 V, inside ±11 V.
Pick Rs. Rs = 1 kΩ limits the high-frequency gain to Rf/Rs = 10 (20 dB) and puts fa at 15.9 kHz.
Compute Cf. Cf = RsC/Rf = 1 nF.
Set Rb = Rf = 10 kΩ.
Select the op-amp. It needs slew rate well above the maximum output slope and a gain–bandwidth product well above fa.
Set the comparator thresholds. About 30–50 % of the expected spike (here ±2 V against a practical spike of about 6.3 V) and several times the measured noise.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages
  • Instant, continuous analog rate-of-change signal.
  • Simple: one op-amp, four passives.
  • Ignores DC and slow drift automatically.
Limitations
  • Amplifies noise, especially at high frequency.
  • Needs Rs, Cf for stability, which limits the useful bandwidth.
  • Output polarity inverted. Amplitude depends on edge speed, so a threshold must be chosen carefully.

Common student misconceptions

  • "The output leads the input by 90°." For the inverting circuit it lags by 90° (equivalently leads by 270°).
  • "DC gives a large output." DC gives zero output; the capacitor blocks it. It is the integrator that has large DC gain.
  • "Rs and Cf are optional extras." Without them a real circuit rings or oscillates.
  • "Output amplitude depends only on input amplitude." It depends on the input slope (amplitude and frequency, or edge time).
  • Swapping R and C. Putting R at the input and C in feedback makes an integrator, not a differentiator.

1. Ideal differentiator: time-domain derivation

Applicable when: ideal op-amp, linear operation, frequencies far below any practical pole.

iC = C · d(Vin − 0)/dt (capacitor, virtual ground)
if = (0 − Vo)/Rf (resistor)
iC = if (no current into the op-amp)
⇒ Vo = − RfC · dVin/dt

Physical meaning: the output measures the slope of the input. Limiting cases: Vin constant gives Vo = 0. A ramp of slope k gives Vo = −RfCk. A step gives an impulse (infinite in theory), which is why a real circuit must limit it.

2. Ideal frequency response

H(s) = Vo/Vin = − sRfC
|H(jω)| = ωRfC = f / f0, f0 = 1/(2πRfC)
∠H(jω) = −90°
Gain in dB = 20 log10(f / f0) (+20 dB/decade)
ff0/10f010 f0100 f0
|H|0.1110100
dB−200+20+40

Sine check: Vin = Vm sin ωt gives Vo = −ωRfC Vm cos ωt = ωRfC Vm sin(ωt − 90°). Peak output = 2πf RfC Vm. The output must stay below Vsat, so fmax = f0 · Vsat/Vm.

3. Practical differentiator: transfer function

Zin = Rs + 1/(sC) = (1 + sRsC)/(sC)
Zf = Rf ∥ 1/(sCf) = Rf/(1 + sRfCf)
H(s) = − Zf/Zin = − sRfC / [(1 + sRsC)(1 + sRfCf)]
|H(jω)| = ωτ1 / √[(1 + ω²τa²)(1 + ω²τb²)]
∠H(jω) = −90° − tan−1(ωτa) − tan−1(ωτb)

Assumptions: ideal op-amp. Limiting cases:

  • ω → 0: H ≈ −sτ1, the ideal differentiator.
  • τb = 0 (no Cf), ω → ∞: |H| → Rf/Rs, an inverting amplifier.
  • With Cf, ω → ∞: |H| → τ1/(ωτaτb), falling at −20 dB/decade.

Matched-pole design (τa = τb = τ2)

Cf = RsC / Rf
|H| = ωτ1 / (1 + ω²τ2²), ∠H = −90° − 2 tan−1(ωτ2)
Peak: ωp = 1/τ2, |H|max = τ1/(2τ2) = Rf/(2Rs)
Accuracy: |H| / |H|ideal = 1/(1 + (f/fa)²)

For the reference design: |H|max = 10 k/(2 × 1 k) = 5 (13.98 dB) at 15.9 kHz. At f = fa/10 the magnitude is 1 % low, but the phase has moved from −90° to −101.4° (−90° − 2 tan−1 0.1), so use f ≪ fa/10 if phase accuracy matters.

4. Time responses of the practical circuit (matched poles)

Step of height A (ideal input step; τ2 = RsC):

Vo(t) = − A (τ1/τ22) · t · e−t/τ2, peak at t = τ2: Vo,pk = − A (Rf/Rs)/e

Ramp of slope a, 0 ≤ t ≤ tr:

Vo(t) = − aτ1 [ 1 − e−t/τ2(1 + t/τ2) ]
Ideal (no poles): Vo = −aτ1 = −RfC·ΔV/tr

Ramp then hold (a finite edge). With n = tr/τ2, the peak occurs slightly after the ramp ends, at x = t/τ2 = n eⁿ/(eⁿ − 1), and

|Vo,pk| = aτ1 [ e−(x−n)(1 + x − n) − e−x(1 + x) ]

For n = 2: x = 2.313 and the bracket = 0.632, so the peak is 0.632 aτ1. The faster the edge compared with τ2 (large n gives longer tr), the closer the peak approaches the ideal aτ1.

5. Design and boundary conditions

No saturation: RfC · ΔV/tr ≲ 0.9 Vsat (ideal estimate)
Sine limit: 2πf RfC Vm ≤ Vsat
Slew rate: SR ≥ max |dVo/dt| (about 0.37 V/µs for the reference ramp response at its steepest point)
Gain–bandwidth: GBW ≫ fa × noise gain at fa
Bias balance: Rb ≈ Rf

DC output: the capacitor is open at DC, so the noise gain is 1: Vo,DC ≈ Vos + Rf(IB− − IB+) ≈ Vos ± IosRf when Rb = Rf.

Power and efficiency: not a design parameter for this small-signal stage. Power dissipation in Rf and the op-amp is small compared with the supply quiescent power, so no power-efficiency equation is used.

Decibel conversion

AdB = 20 log10|H| ⇔ |H| = 10AdB/20, e.g. 20 dB ↔ 10, 14 dB ≈ 5, 6 dB ≈ 2, 3 dB ≈ 1.414

Solved numerical problems

Easy Example 1: sine input

Given: ideal differentiator, Rf = 10 kΩ, C = 0.1 µF, Vin = 0.5 sin(2π·500 t) V. Find: Vo(t).

Formula: Vo = −RfC dVin/dt.

Substitute: RfC = 10×10³ × 0.1×10⁻⁶ = 1 ms. ω = 2π × 500 = 3141.6 rad/s. dVin/dt = 0.5 × 3141.6 cos(3141.6 t) = 1570.8 cos(3141.6 t) V/s.

Calculate: Vo = −(1×10⁻³)(1570.8) cos(3141.6 t) = −1.571 cos(2π·500 t) V.

Interpretation: the amplitude is 1.571 V, which is ωRfC × 0.5 = 3.14 × 0.5. It equals 1.571 sin(ωt − 90°), so the output lags the input by 90°. Here f0 = 1/(2π × 1 ms) = 159 Hz, so 500 Hz is above f0 and the gain is 500/159 = 3.14 > 1.

Moderate Example 2 (GATE-style): triangle input

Given: ideal differentiator with Rf = 1 kΩ, C = 0.1 µF. Input: symmetrical triangle wave, ±1 V peak, period 2 ms. Find: the output waveform and amplitude.

Formula: Vo = −RfC × slope.

Substitute: RfC = 10³ × 10⁻⁷ = 0.1 ms. Rise: 2 V in 1 ms, slope = +2000 V/s. Fall: slope = −2000 V/s.

Calculate: Vo = −(10⁻⁴)(2000) = −0.2 V during the rise and +0.2 V during the fall.

Answer: a square wave of ±0.2 V (0.4 V peak-to-peak), 2 ms period, 180° inverted relative to the rising slope.

Shortcut: amplitude = RfC × (Vpp/half-period) = 10⁻⁴ × (2/10⁻³) = 0.2 V. Triangle in, square out, always.

Moderate Example 3: design for a ramp

Given: a ramp rises at 2 V/ms. The output must be a constant −4 V. Choose C = 0.1 µF. Find: Rf.

Formula: Vo = −RfC k, with k = 2 V/ms = 2000 V/s.

Calculate: RfC = 4/2000 = 2 ms. Rf = 2×10⁻³/0.1×10⁻⁶ = 20 kΩ.

Interpretation: a constant output is a direct measurement of a constant slope, so a ramp converts a rate into a DC level. Set Rb = 20 kΩ and add Rs, Cf in a real build.

Moderate Example 4: practical circuit gain and phase at 5 kHz

Given: Rs = 1 kΩ, C = 10 nF, Rf = 10 kΩ, Cf = 1 nF. Find: |H| in dB and the phase at f = 5 kHz, and compare with the ideal circuit.

Time constants: τ1 = 10 kΩ × 10 nF = 100 µs. τ2 = RsC = RfCf = 10 µs. ω = 2π × 5000 = 31,416 rad/s.

Substitute: ωτ1 = 3.1416. ωτ2 = 0.31416. 1 + (ωτ2)² = 1.0987.

Calculate: |H| = 3.1416/1.0987 = 2.859, so 20 log10(2.859) = 9.13 dB. Phase = −90° − 2 tan−1(0.31416) = −90° − 34.9° = −124.9°.

Interpretation: the ideal gain would be 3.1416 (9.94 dB, −90°). The practical gain is 9 % low and the phase has moved by 35°, because 5 kHz is already about fa/3. The circuit is no longer a faithful differentiator at 5 kHz, which is fine for edge detection but not for precise calculus.

Difficult Example 5: the edge detector (design check)

Given: reference design (Rf = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF, Rs = 1 kΩ, Cf = 1 nF), ±12 V supply (Vsat ≈ ±11 V), input edge of ΔV = 2 V in tr = 20 µs. Find: (a) ideal spike, (b) practical spike, (c) whether it saturates, (d) a suitable threshold.

(a) a = 2 V/20 µs = 10⁵ V/s. Ideal |Vo| = RfC a = 100 µs × 10⁵ = 10 V. This is below 11 V, so no clipping, but with only 10 % margin.

(b) τ2 = 10 µs, so n = tr/τ2 = 2. aτ1 = 10 V. At the end of the ramp the output is 10 × [1 − e⁻²(1 + 2)] = 10 × 0.594 = 5.94 V. The true peak occurs at x = 2e²/(e² − 1) = 2.313, i.e. t = 23.1 µs, where the bracket is 0.632. Peak = 6.32 V.

(c) 6.32 V < 11 V, so the output stays linear. The polarity is negative for the rising edge and positive for the falling edge.

(d) Choose |Vth| ≈ 2 V (about one third of the spike). Allow for noise: with the gain peaking at 5, a 50 mV p-p noise at the input could add up to about 0.25 V at the output, well below 2 V.

Interpretation: the poles reduce the spike from 10 V to 6.3 V, but they also tame noise and ringing. The designer trades spike size against robustness.

Difficult Example 6 (GATE-style): maximum frequency before clipping

Given: ideal differentiator with RfC = 100 µs, Vin = 2 sin ωt V, output saturation ±11 V. Find: the highest frequency that can be processed without clipping.

Formula: Vo,pk = ωRfC Vm ≤ Vsat.

Calculate: ωmax = 11/(2 × 10⁻⁴) = 55,000 rad/s. fmax = 55,000/(2π) = 8.75 kHz.

Shortcut: |H| = f/f0. f0 = 1/(2π × 100 µs) = 1.5915 kHz. Allowed gain = Vsat/Vm = 5.5, so fmax = 5.5 × 1.5915 kHz = 8.75 kHz.

Interpretation: a differentiator's output limit depends on frequency, not only on amplitude. Higher-frequency or larger signals clip first.

Core electronics company interview preparation

Click a question to open a concise answer. Follow-up questions an interviewer may ask are included. Roles: analog design, hardware testing and validation, electronics R&D, and instrumentation.

1. What does an op-amp differentiator do, and why is it called inverting? Fundamental

It outputs Vo = −RfC dVin/dt. The capacitor current C dVin/dt flows through Rf into the output with virtual ground at the inverting node, so the output polarity is opposite to the input slope.

Follow-up: What is the phase for a sine input? The output lags by 90° (−j ωRfC).

2. Why can't you use the ideal differentiator in a real design? Why

Its gain rises forever (+20 dB/decade), so high-frequency noise dominates. The noise gain 1 + sRfC also meets the op-amp's falling open-loop gain at a 40 dB/decade closing slope, leaving almost no phase margin, so it rings or oscillates. The capacitor also loads the source heavily at high frequency, and a very fast edge saturates the output.

Follow-up: What is the minimum fix? A resistor in series with the capacitor (limits gain to Rf/Rs), then Cf across Rf for roll-off.

3. How do you choose Rs and Cf? Design

Pick fa = 1/(2πRsC) about 5–10× above the highest frequency you want to differentiate accurately (for pure edge detection, make τ2 = RsC comparable to or shorter than the edge time). Then set Cf = RsC/Rf so the poles coincide at fa. The peak gain is Rf/(2Rs). For Rf = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF, Rs = 1 kΩ: fa = 15.9 kHz, Cf = 1 nF, peak gain 5.

Follow-up: What if you make Rs larger? Lower noise and more stability, but smaller spikes and a lower fa.

4. Turn the differentiator output into a clean digital edge flag. Design

Use a window comparator: one comparator for +Vth and one for −Vth, optionally with hysteresis, combined with an OR or SR latch. The inverting stage gives a negative spike for a rising edge and a positive spike for a falling edge, so the two comparators also tell the edge direction. Set Vth above the noise and below the smallest spike you must detect. A one-shot or MCU interrupt can stretch the flag.

Follow-up: What happens if two edges are very close? The spike tails overlap and may merge; limit it by τ2 shorter than the minimum edge spacing.

5. Troubleshooting: the output shows ringing or sustained oscillation on a differentiator. What do you check? Debug

(1) Is Rs present, and is it large enough? (2) Is Cf present and soldered correctly? (3) Is the op-amp's phase margin low (decoupling, long leads, probe capacitance, parasitic capacitance at the inverting node)? (4) Is the op-amp stable for this noise gain (unity-gain stable?). Fix: increase Rs and Cf, add 100 nF decoupling at the supply pins, shorten the inverting-node leads, and use a faster or compensated op-amp.

6. Lab: how do you verify a differentiator on the bench? Lab

Apply a triangle wave: the output should be a square wave of amplitude RfC × slope. Then apply a sine and check amplitude ∝ frequency and a −90° shift (lags). Sweep the frequency to confirm the +20 dB/decade rise and the roll-off at fa. Use DC coupling and keep the input small enough that the output does not clip. Check the spikes on a square wave and measure the peak against the prediction.

Follow-up: Why is a triangle wave a good test signal? Its slope is constant, so the expected output is simple and easy to measure.

7. Multisim/SPICE: which analyses would you run, and what model pitfalls exist? Simulation

AC sweep for the Bode plot (compare with H(s)), transient for edge response, and a noise or parametric sweep for Rs and Cf. Pitfalls: ideal (virtual) op-amps hide slew-rate, GBW and output-swing limits; generator edge time may be zero, giving unrealistic spikes; too large a time step misses the spike peak; and the model must be powered to show saturation.

8. How do GBW and slew rate limit this circuit? Analog

The spike requires the output to move quickly. If its slope exceeds SR, the output is slew-limited and the spike is distorted and late. GBW limits the loop gain at the frequencies where the spike has energy, so the virtual-ground assumption fails near fa and above, adding extra delay and peaking. Choose an op-amp with SR much larger than max |dVo/dt| and GBW well above fa times the noise gain.

9. Why not just use a passive RC high-pass filter as the differentiator? Compare

A passive RC high-pass approximates differentiation only when f ≪ 1/(2πRC), where its output is tiny (gain ≪ 1) and loaded by whatever follows. The active circuit has gain, a low output impedance and a virtual-ground input so the source sees Rs + capacitor and the output can drive a comparator directly. The price is noise gain and stability design.

10. Why is Rb placed on the non-inverting input, and what is the DC output? Fundamental

At DC the capacitor is open, so the inverting-node DC resistance is Rf. Making Rb = Rf balances the bias-current drops, leaving the DC output ≈ Vos ± IosRf. The noise gain at DC is 1, so offsets are not amplified, unlike in an integrator.

Follow-up: Does the differentiator need a DC-reset? No; the integrator does.

11. How do you reduce noise sensitivity further? Follow-up

Lower the pole frequencies (larger Rs, larger Cf), add a second-order low-pass before or after the stage, use a low-noise op-amp, shield and keep leads short, or use a digital approach (ADC and finite differences with filtering) if the bandwidth allows.

12. Design scenario: detect a 3.3 V logic edge with 1 µs transition and trigger a comparator at 1 V. Design

Slope = 3.3 V/1 µs = 3.3×10⁶ V/s. For an ideal peak of about 2 V: RfC ≈ 2/3.3×10⁶ = 0.6 µs, e.g. C = 100 pF, Rf = 6.2 kΩ. Keep τ2 = RsC about 0.1 µs, so Rs = 1 kΩ, and Cf = RsC/Rf ≈ 16 pF (15 pF). Use a fast op-amp (GBW in the hundreds of MHz, high SR) and a fast comparator with a 1 V threshold. At this speed a digital delay-and-XOR edge detector may be the better engineering choice.

Practical industry perspective

In real products an analog differentiator is rarely a stand-alone ideal block. It appears as a band-limited rate-of-change stage (the derivative term in analog controllers, slope or dV/dt sensing in power and instrumentation circuits, pulse or trigger shaping) always with the roll-off and gain limit shown here. Test and validation engineers check it with a function generator, a scope with bandwidth well above the edge, and a Bode sweep. Designers also check worst-case noise, saturation and temperature variation of R and C.

Common implementation and test challenges

  • Noise and oscillation when Rs or Cf is omitted or too small.
  • Spike amplitude varies with edge rate, so thresholds need margin and may need tuning.
  • Output saturation on unexpectedly fast edges; slew-rate limiting of cheap op-amps.
  • Capacitor tolerance, dielectric and temperature drift shift the gain and fa; use C0G/NP0 or film capacitors for stable timing.
  • Probe loading and ground leads can distort fast spikes during measurement.

Career relevance

Analog design, hardware validation, instrumentation and R&D roles expect you to explain the practical differentiator, justify Rs and Cf, estimate noise and stability, and propose a debugging plan. The same ideas (noise gain, phase margin, band-limiting) carry over to PID design, active filters and transimpedance amplifiers.

Quick-revision notes

  • Differentiator = C at input, R in feedback. Integrator = R at input, C in feedback.
  • DC → 0. Ramp → DC. Triangle → square. Sine → cosine (lag 90°). Square → spikes.
  • Rising edge → negative spike, falling edge → positive spike (inverting circuit).
  • Add Rs and Cf for stability. Differentiate only for f ≪ fa.

Memory aid: "C comes in, R feeds back, D for Derivative." If the capacitor is at the input, the circuit reacts to change.

Important definitions

  • Differentiator: circuit whose output ∝ dVin/dt.
  • Virtual ground: inverting node held near 0 V by feedback.
  • Noise gain: 1 + Zf/Zin; governs offsets and stability.
  • Edge detection: deriving an event from a rapid change in a signal.

Advantages and disadvantages (summary)

Advantages: simple, instant rate-of-change signal, ignores DC. Disadvantages: noise sensitivity, stability constraints, output depends on edge speed, inverted polarity.
